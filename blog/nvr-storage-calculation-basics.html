NVR Storage Calculation Basics

A buyer-friendly workflow so last Tuesday’s incident footage is actually there.

NVR storage calculation is where CCTV projects become real. Camera count and megapixels look impressive in a proposal; hard-disk capacity and retention days decide whether security can actually retrieve last Tuesday’s incident. This article explains a practical estimation method for industrial and commercial buyers in India — useful for factories and campuses in Jharkhand and Bihar planning NVR purchases with ATRICL or any supplier.

What drives storage

  • Number of cameras recording
  • Resolution and scene complexity (busy gates need more bits than empty corridors)
  • Frames per second (FPS)
  • Compression / codec efficiency and quality presets
  • Recording mode: continuous vs motion-based
  • Retention days required by management or compliance policy
  • Audio streams, if enabled

Vendor datasheets sometimes publish bitrate tables. Use them as a starting point, then add margin. Real sites with monsoon clouds, headlights at gates, and dusty yards do not match laboratory scenes.

A simple estimation workflow

  1. List cameras by zone and intended resolution.
  2. Pick a bitrate assumption per camera from manufacturer guidance for that resolution/FPS/quality — or measure on a pilot camera.
  3. Convert bitrate to GB/day: roughly, Mbps × 10.8 ≈ GB/day per stream (because 1 Mbps ≈ 10.8 GB/day continuous). Confirm with your vendor’s calculator when available.
  4. Multiply by camera count (or sum different bitrates).
  5. Multiply by retention days.
  6. Add 15–30% headroom for growth, formatting overhead and unexpected bitrate spikes.
  7. Select HDD count/capacity supported by the NVR (and use surveillance-rated disks as recommended by the NVR maker).

Example thinking (illustrative only, not a quote): 16 cameras averaging a moderate bitrate, 15 days retention, continuous recording — storage climbs quickly into multiple terabytes. Always recalculate with your actual models.

Motion recording vs continuous

Motion-based recording saves disk but risks gaps if sensitivity is wrong or if plants want uninterrupted evidence. Many industrial security teams prefer continuous recording on gates and cash/material areas, with motion elsewhere. Document the policy; do not leave it to installer defaults.

RAID and disk failure reality

Not every NVR supports RAID. If a single disk holds critical evidence, plan backups or shorter retention with export procedures. Hot environments in electrical rooms shorten disk life — place NVRs in ventilated, accessible locations, not above a hot panel.

Network and PoE budgeting belong in the same meeting

Storage sizing without switch PoE budget is incomplete. Confirm that the NVR uplink and camera switches can handle sustained bitrate. Oversubscribed cheap switches drop packets and create mysterious “recording gaps.”

Retention policy questions for management

  • How many days must we keep gate footage?
  • Are there investigations that need 30+ days?
  • Who is authorised to export clips?
  • What happens when disks fill — overwrite oldest or stop recording? (Usually overwrite — confirm.)

FAQ

Can I use desktop hard disks?

Follow the NVR manufacturer’s guidance. Surveillance workloads are write-heavy; wrong disks fail early.

Does higher megapixel always need much more storage?

Often yes at the same compression quality, but codec, FPS and quality presets matter. Do not assume linear scaling without checking.

How often should we verify recording?

Weekly spot-checks of random cameras and last-night footage beat discovering gaps after an incident.

Worked planning conversation (illustrative)

Suppose a plant wants 24 outdoor + 8 indoor cameras, 20 days retention, continuous on 10 gate/perimeter cameras and motion on the rest. You would estimate bitrates separately for the continuous group and the motion group (motion needs an assumed duty cycle — be conservative). Sum GB/day, multiply by 20, add headroom, then pick an NVR that supports the camera count, throughput and disk bays. If the NVR throughput rating is below total bitrate, storage maths will not save you — the recorder becomes the bottleneck.

Always validate with the chosen camera’s bitrate at night scenes; IR noise can raise bitrate versus daytime assumptions.

Export and evidence handling

Teach shift in-charges how to export a clip with timestamp. Untested export workflows are discovered during incidents — the worst time. Keep a USB policy and a short SOP near the NVR.

Bitrate measurement on a pilot camera

If manufacturer tables feel optimistic, mount one camera of the intended model at a real gate for 24–48 hours and read average bitrate from the NVR or switch tools. Night averages often exceed day averages. Multiply that measured average by camera count and retention days, then add headroom.

This pilot costs little compared with buying undersized disks twice.

Multi-site and central viewing

If management wants to view multiple plants from a central office, storage may remain local while viewing is remote — or footage may be centralised. Those architectures change bandwidth and disk design. State the requirement early; do not discover it after NVRs are purchased.
