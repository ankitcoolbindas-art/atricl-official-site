Factory Perimeter CCTV Planning

Updated 27 Sep 2026

See the boundary early enough to respond — with overlaps, power and recording that work at night.

Factory perimeter CCTV planning is about seeing the boundary early enough to respond — not about sprinkling cameras until the quote looks large. This practical guide is for plant heads, security officers and electrical contractors laying out perimeter coverage for industrial sites in India, including facilities in and around Ranchi, Jharkhand and Bihar.

Walk the fence line with a map

Start with a scaled site plan. Mark:

  • Main and secondary gates
  • Weak fence sections and drainage culverts
  • Material yards open to the boundary
  • Dark vegetation or unused structures that block views
  • Existing poles, buildings and cable routes you can reuse

A daytime walk plus a night walk changes priorities. Headlights, street spill and pitch-dark stretches behave differently after sunset.

Detection vs recognition vs identification

Be honest about what each camera must do. Detection (“something moved”) can use wider views. Recognition / identification needs tighter fields of view and better lighting. Putting one ultra-wide camera on a 200-metre fence and expecting number-plate-level detail is a design failure, not a product failure.

Camera placement patterns that work

  • Gates: dedicated cameras for approach lane, stop line and guard interaction — do not rely only on a distant PTZ.
  • Fence lines: overlapping fixed views along the boundary; avoid huge gaps between fields of view.
  • Corners: corners create blind angles — plan overlaps deliberately.
  • Yards: cover stock piles that sit near the fence; theft routes often follow material, not the shortest geometric path.

PTZ cameras help operators patrol, but they should not be the only coverage for a critical segment. When a PTZ looks left, the right side is blind unless another camera covers it.

Analytics — useful, not magical

Line-crossing and intrusion rules can reduce operator fatigue when tuned carefully. They also create false alarms from animals, moving vegetation and headlights if sensitivity and masking are ignored. Plan a tuning period after installation. ATRICL will not claim AI eliminates guarding needs; treat analytics as an assistant.

Lighting and infrared

Perimeter cameras without adequate lighting produce noisy night images and inflate storage bitrates. Combine IR / supplemental lighting with sensible mounting heights. Avoid pointing cameras directly into opposing floodlights.

Power, network and earthing on the perimeter

Outdoor runs need:

  • Thoughtful power distribution and isolation
  • Surge protection practise suitable for exposed poles
  • Cable routes that maintenance can reach without cutting production
  • Network design that does not daisy-chain fragile consumer switches across a kilometre of fence

Coordinate with the electrical team early. Perimeter CCTV is an outdoor electrical project as much as a security project.

Recording and response workflow

Coverage is useless without a response path. Define who watches live views, who gets alarm notifications, and how clips are exported for management or police. Size NVR storage for perimeter cameras that often run continuous recording (see NVR storage basics).

Phased deployment for brownfield plants

  1. Secure gates and known incident hotspots first.
  2. Close long fence gaps next.
  3. Add analytics after stable picture quality exists.
  4. Document channel maps and passwords in a controlled handover file.

  • Existing cameras / poles

FAQ

How many cameras do I need per 100 metres of fence?

There is no universal number — lens, height and required detail decide. Design overlaps for your goal (detect vs identify).

Are wireless cameras good for perimeters?

Sometimes for temporary coverage; for permanent industrial boundaries, prefer engineered cabled networks unless RF survey and power are solid.

Should guards be removed after CCTV?

No. CCTV supports people; it does not replace access control discipline and guard procedures.

Integrating perimeter CCTV with electrical infrastructure

Perimeter projects fail when camera poles are erected without cable ducts, or when power is tapped from random lighting circuits. Give CCTV its planned feeders and isolation. Label outdoor junction boxes. Keep as-built drawings updated — the next shutdown team should not reverse-engineer the fence.

If you are already upgrading yard lighting, co-plan camera brackets and cable trays. Shared access equipment saves cost; shared messy wiring creates faults that are hard to isolate.

Handover package (minimum)

  1. Channel list with camera location names matching the site map
  2. Admin and operator passwords stored per plant IT policy
  3. NVR retention setting screenshot
  4. Spare camera / PSU list
  5. Warranty and invoice references for accounts

Ask for this package in your purchase order language so it is not “forgotten” at the end.

False alarm management

If analytics flood the control room, operators ignore alarms. Use schedules (armed at night), exclude zones for trees, and review weekly false-alarm top sources. Combine CCTV with physical measures: fence repair, lighting, and gate discipline.

Document which alarms require immediate patrol versus log-only. Clear SOPs matter more than another camera.

Legal and workplace considerations

Inform employees appropriately about workplace CCTV per your HR/legal guidance. Avoid cameras in prohibited private areas. Perimeter focus is usually less contentious than shop-floor privacy debates, but still document purpose: safety and asset protection.
